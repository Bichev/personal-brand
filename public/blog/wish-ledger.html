The Wish Ledger
You Live Inside Your Ancestors' Fairy Tales

Flying carpets, mirrors that answer, servants made of bronze, water that heals. I collected 125 wishes from the myths and fairy tales of more than 40 peoples, sorted them into ten kinds and checked each one against the record. Most have been granted, usually to everybody. Nine things to play with, and a source behind every link.

I. The Translation

Your morning, told to someone from 1300

Pick one of your ancestors from around the year 1300. It does not matter where she lived. She almost certainly works the land. She has probably buried a child, because for most of history close to half of all children died before they grew up. Her evenings are lit by a fire, and the furthest she has ever heard a human voice carry is across a field.

Now tell her about your morning. Say only what happened, without explaining how any of it works.

A slab of glass woke you with music played by people who were not in the room. You asked it a question out loud and it answered. You touched the wall and the room turned as bright as noon. Breakfast came from three continents and was already in the house. You looked into the glass and saw your sister, who lives across an ocean, and she heard you laugh.

She has no idea of the future to file this under, so she will take it for fairy tales. She will recognise every one of them, because she already knows them: the mirror that answers, the cloth that lays its own table, the carpet, the water of life.

Arthur C. Clarke wrote in 1973 that "any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic", and the line has been repeated ever since. I wanted to check a narrower claim, one that can be audited. The specific things people asked for in their oldest stories are, item by item, the things now lying around the house. So I made a list.

II. The Ledger

What people asked for

The audit had three rules.

A tale counts as a wish and never as a prediction. Nobody who told the story of Icarus was forecasting aviation. They were saying that people want to fly, and that is the only claim I make for any tale here, including the flying palace in the Ramayana, which is sometimes presented as an ancient aircraft.

Each tale is dated by its earliest written source, so every story is older than the date beside it. The Russian flying carpet is in print from 1794 and was told long before that. Baba Yaga flew in her mortar for centuries before Alexander Afanasyev collected her in the 1850s. For peoples whose stories were first written down by a visiting ethnographer, the date says more about the visitor than about the story.

A weak match was left out, and so was anything I could not trace to a source. That cost me some favourites. The cap of invisibility did not fit any of the ten wishes and waits in section IX. Alexander the Great's diving bell has no origin I could date. And the Chinese official Wan Hu, who is said to have tied 47 rockets to a chair, turns out to appear first in an American magazine in 1909. He is in the ledger with a warning label.

What remained is 125 tales and objects from more than 40 peoples. They sorted themselves into ten wishes with very little forcing.

An empty cell means I found no match strong enough to keep, which is different from that people never having made the wish. Rows marked REAL are working techniques or machines, included where a people skipped the wish and built the thing.

Tales that read like product descriptions

In a Russian tale printed in 1838, a girl rolls a ripe apple around a silver saucer, says a rhyme, and the saucer shows her cities, ships at sea and regiments on the march. Later the tsar is asked what he would like to see. English readers met it in 1916 through Arthur Ransome's Old Peter's Russian Tales.

In the Arabian Nights, three princes go shopping. Ali buys an ivory tube a foot long, with glass at each end, that shows whatever its owner wishes to see. Houssain buys a carpet that takes you anywhere at once. Ahmed buys an artificial apple that cures any illness. Ali looks through the tube and sees the princess dying, Houssain flies all three brothers to her, Ahmed cures her, and the Sultan cannot decide who has earned her hand, because the rescue fails if you remove any one of the three. It is a fair description of a video consultation, an air ambulance and a pharmacy, and it was told in Paris on 22 May 1709 by a Syrian traveller named Hanna Diyab.

The Ebony Horse, in Arabic manuscript since the 1230s, is a mechanical horse with a pin on the right shoulder to climb and a pin on the left to come down. The prince takes off before the inventor has explained the second pin. Anyone who has been handed new software on a Friday afternoon will recognise the scene.

Homer gives the Phaeacians ships with no helmsman, which know every city and understand what the crew intends. A Welsh tale has a people who hear every word the wind touches, so that two kings can only plan against them by speaking through a long bronze horn. That is a surveillance problem solved with a secure line, written down about eight hundred years ago.

If every tool could do its own work when ordered, if the shuttle wove and the plectrum touched the lyre without a hand to guide them, chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves. Aristotle, Politics, Book 1, about 322 BCE (after Jowett's translation, shortened)

Aristotle was thinking of the self-moving tripods that Hephaestus builds in the Iliad, and he meant the sentence as an impossibility. It took about 2,100 years to become an economic forecast.

The Nights tales that everybody knows, Aladdin and the flying carpet, are not in the Arabic manuscripts at all, which I had not known. They entered the book in French, from Diyab's telling. The tales that are in the Arabic are the ones full of machinery: a City of Brass where a brass horseman turns and points the road when you rub his hand, a brass boatman who rows for days without tiring, and a fisherman who rubs on an ointment and walks about on the sea floor.

III. The Map

Which peoples shared which wishes

Put the tales on a map and no wish stays inside one culture. The flying carpet I grew up thinking of as Russian is also Arabian, and the self-setting tablecloth has a German cousin, an Irish one and a Sanskrit one.

Here are eight wishes with the versions that sit nearest to each other, and what finally answered them.

The Russian living water and the Hawaiian Water of Life of Kāne were written down sixty years apart, on opposite sides of the planet, by people who had never heard of each other. The K'iche' Maya and the Quechua of Huarochirí both recorded a day when the grinding stones and cooking pots rose against their owners, and so, in his way, did Lucian, who wrote the first sorcerer's apprentice around 150 CE. The wish for servants that are made rather than born seems to arrive everywhere with the same worry attached.

Three peoples who skipped the wish and built the thing

Not every row in the ledger is a tale. In the 1730s Francis Moore noted a Mandinka town drum that could be heard six or seven miles away at night. It took an outsider until 1949 to explain in print how Kele drummers send whole sentences between villages: the language is tonal, so a drum with two pitches can carry the melody of a sentence, and stock phrases remove the ambiguity. It is long-distance speech, and it was working before the telegraph.

In 1769 the navigator Tupaia drew James Cook a chart of 74 islands from memory. He knew about 130. Polynesian wayfinders crossed open ocean with no instruments by reading stars, swells, winds and birds, and in 1976 the canoe Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti the same way to settle the argument. The Inca ran relay runners who could bring fresh sea fish to Cusco, and kept a census in knotted cords. The runners did the work of seven-league boots, and the cords did the work of an archive.

IV. The Wait

How long a wish waits

Etana, a king of Kish, asks an eagle to carry him up to heaven on a clay tablet from about 1750 BCE. He panics on the way and turns back. The first people to leave the ground and come home alive did it over Paris in November 1783. That wish waited about 3,500 years.

Jules Verne sent three men around the Moon from Florida in 1865. Apollo 8 did it with three men from Florida in 1968. Arthur C. Clarke described the communications satellite in 1945 and Syncom 3 was relaying the Tokyo Olympics in 1964. I lined up 39 wishes and visions this way, from the oldest tablet to a 1992 novel.

Visual 04  /  The Lag
Hollow dot: the year the wish was written down. Green dot: the year something real answered it. The number is the wait in years. The time axis is stretched towards the present, or the last two centuries would be a smudge. Tap a row for what answered it.
I picked these rows. Wishes that never came true are missing by construction, and they get their own section at the end. The shrinking wait describes this list and may not hold beyond it.

My reading of why is that the people writing the wishes down changed. For three thousand years they were poets and grandmothers. From about 1600 they were increasingly people who could do the arithmetic, and the wish started to arrive with a sketch attached. The sketches then found readers. Leo Szilard read H. G. Wells's atomic bombs in 1932 and conceived the chain reaction in 1933. Douglas Engelbart read Vannevar Bush's memex essay in 1945 and went on to build the mouse and the hyperlink. Apollo 11's command module was named Columbia partly after Verne's cannon, the Columbiad. Engineers at NASA called their remote manipulators waldoes, after a 1942 Heinlein story. By the twentieth century engineers were reading the stories and building from them.

V. The Sketches

The scientists who wrote it down

Between the storytellers and the engineers stand a few people who treated the wish as a design problem before anyone could build it. Around 1260 a Franciscan friar in Oxford wrote a letter listing machines he thought possible. Around 1627 a disgraced Lord Chancellor described a research institute with laboratories for sound, optics, flight and the prolongation of life, and thirty-three years later the founders of the Royal Society treated his fable as a plan.

Visual 05  /  On the record
Twelve thinkers and engineers, what they wrote, and how it turned out. Text in italics is quoted exactly from a source published before 1930. Plain text is my paraphrase of a later work.
I graded mechanism as well as outcome. Roger Bacon's flying machine is hand-cranked and Leonardo's aerial screw would not have left the ground, so both are marked partial. The parachute Leonardo drew was built to his sketch in 2000, and the jump succeeded.

My favourite entry is Ada Lovelace, because she is right in both directions inside one set of notes. In 1843 she wrote that Babbage's engine "might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent". A few pages later she wrote that it "has no pretensions whatever to originate any thing". We are still arguing about which of the two sentences describes the machines of the 2020s.

Experts who said never

A fair audit needs the opposite column as well. These are the well-sourced cases of qualified people ruling something out, with the time it took to be wrong. I left out the famous fakes. Lord Kelvin's "heavier-than-air flying machines are impossible" has no primary source, so the table uses a letter he did write. IBM's Thomas Watson never said the world needed five computers.

The naysayers
Sorted by how quickly events answered.
On 12 September 1933 Leo Szilard read Rutherford's remark in The Times, was annoyed, and worked out the neutron chain reaction while waiting at a traffic light on Southampton Row. The record for this table is one day.

The New York Times holds two places. On 9 October 1903 it estimated that a flying machine might be evolved "in from one million to ten million years". The Wright brothers flew 69 days later. In 1920 it told Robert Goddard that he lacked "the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools" for thinking a rocket could work in a vacuum. On 17 July 1969, with Apollo 11 on its way to the Moon, the paper printed a short notice that ends: "The Times regrets the error."

VI. The Fantasts

What the science-fiction writers got right

Russian calls a science-fiction writer a fantast, which I think is the better word. The twentieth century had hundreds of them and I checked the ones whose claims could be traced to the text. Two nineteenth-century forerunners open the table, because one of them is Russian and almost unknown in English.

In 1835 Prince Vladimir Odoevsky began a novel set in the year 4338. In it, friends' houses are linked by "magnetic telegraphs", and households issue their own home newspapers to acquaintances in place of letters, with news of the family's health, thoughts and dinner invitations. He gave it 2,500 years. The telephone took 41 and the blog about 164.

Visual 06  /  The Fantasts' Scorecard
Tap a column heading to sort. "Years off" is the gap between publication and arrival. HIT means the thing arrived roughly as described. PARTIAL means the idea arrived and the mechanism was wrong. OPEN means not yet.
Works after 1930 are described in my own words. I dropped several names I wanted (Efremov, Tezuka, Philip K. Dick) because I could not verify a clean claim, and one legend its own hero took back: Martin Cooper, who made the first mobile phone call in 1973, said in 2021 that his inspiration "wasn't Star Trek, it was Dick Tracy".

E. M. Forster, a novelist of Edwardian manners, wrote one science-fiction story in 1909. Its people live alone in small rooms, meet only through screens, and trade second-hand ideas, all served by one global Machine. He meant it as a warning and it read like reportage in the spring of 2020.

Murray Leinster, in March 1946, when the world had a handful of computers, put a "logic" in every home: a keyboard and a screen wired to central tanks, answering any question. In the story one of them loses its censorship filters and starts helpfully telling people how to commit crimes. That is the personal computer, the search engine and the jailbroken chatbot in one short story, with seventy-six years of lead on the last of the three.

And Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain, writing in English in Madras in 1905, imagined a country run by women that collects "as much sun-heat as they wanted", tills its fields by electricity and travels by air-car. She was twenty-five. The Wrights had flown two years earlier and almost nobody had heard.

The fantasts were mostly right about information and mostly wrong about transport and energy. The pocket library, the wall screen and the video call arrived. The flying car and the Moon colony did not, and neither did the meal in a pill.

VII. The King's Ledger

Richer than a king

Nathan Mayer Rothschild was, by most accounts, the richest man in the world in the summer of 1836. He travelled to Frankfurt for his son's wedding, an abscess became infected, and he was dead on 28 July at fifty-eight. Every doctor money could summon attended him. None of them had anything that worked.

The oldest tales ask for the carpet and the mirror, but far more of them ask for a full table, a child who lives, and a body that mends. Those wishes can be measured, so here are the numbers.

Visual 07  /  The Year Dial
Drag the dial through seven centuries. Each tile shows the best available figure for an ordinary person in that year. Bars show the same number relative to the best year on record.

One correction before anybody quotes the first tile at a dinner party. A life expectancy of thirty never meant that people died at thirty. It is an average dragged down by dead children. In Sweden in 1751 a newborn could expect 38 years, but a fifteen-year-old could expect to reach 58. The old world had plenty of grandmothers and no safe childhood: in the historical record about a quarter of babies died in their first year and about half of all children before fifteen. Today a Swedish fifteen-year-old can expect to reach 83, so adults gained a quarter of a century as well.

Light is my favourite tile because it is so ordinary. William Nordhaus worked out what an evening of decent light cost in hours of labour. In Babylon in 1750 BCE, with a sesame-oil lamp, it was 41 hours. In 1800, with tallow candles, a little over five. By 1992 it was under half a second. In money terms lighting in Britain is about 14,000 times cheaper than it was in 1300. Māui had to beat the sun into submission to lengthen the day, and we do it with a switch.

What money could not buy

People usually compare themselves with a medieval peasant. The kings and bankers below are a harder test, because they had everything their age could offer.

They had the fastest horses, the best cooks and the most learned physicians alive. A king's messenger was the quickest thing on Earth, and news of Lincoln's death still took twelve days to reach London in 1865, because the Atlantic cable would not work for another fifteen months. They could not buy the apple that cures, the mirror that shows or the voice from far away, and you were issued all three at birth.

The wish was not granted evenly. About one person in ten still lives in extreme poverty. 655 million people have no electricity. In Nigeria food takes 59% of household spending and in the United States 6.8%.

VIII. Rhymes and Fine Print

Old intuitions that rhyme

So far everything in the ledger has been a thing: a carpet, a mirror, a servant. Some entries are ideas, and those are harder to audit. The Dao and quantum physics is the famous example, and the careless version of it is all over the internet.

In 1947 the King of Denmark awarded Niels Bohr the Order of the Elephant, an honour normally kept for royalty, which requires a coat of arms. Bohr had none, so he designed one. He put the yin-yang in the middle of it, red and black, and under it the motto Contraria sunt complementa: opposites are complementary. The man who had spent twenty years explaining that light is a wave and a particle, and that you can see either face but never both at once, looked for a picture of his idea and found that the best one available had been drawn in China.

The story is true, and it is as far as the evidence goes. Bohr presented complementarity in 1927 and picked the emblem twenty years later, so he did not get the idea from Laozi. Physics does not prove the Dao, and the Dao predicted no experiment. The record supports a smaller claim, which I like better: people thinking hard about the world more than two thousand years apart sometimes arrive at the same shape of thought.

Visual 08  /  The Resonance Cards
Old ideas set beside the modern finding they are usually compared with. For each one: what the record supports, and where popular accounts overreach.

The I Ching story has the same structure. The hexagrams really do count from 0 to 63 in binary if you read them the right way, and Leibniz really was delighted when the diagram reached him from Beijing in 1703. He had also invented binary twenty-four years earlier, so the old text matched his mathematics without being its source.

The warnings in the same tales

The same people who recorded the wishes usually recorded the price. I think this part of the old stories has aged best, and it is why I call this article an audit.

The fine print
Tales that recorded the price of a wish, and the modern echo.

The Grimms' porridge pot and Lucian's enchanted pestle are the same bug report, sixteen centuries apart: the thing does exactly what it was told, and nobody present knows the word for stop. In the Popol Vuh the gods make the first men so well that they can see everything on Earth, near and far. The gods decide this is too much like being gods, and breathe a mist over human eyes, the way breath clouds a mirror. I read that as the wish for the saucer and the apple, told from the other side.

IX. The Open Ledger

Wishes still waiting

The ledger also has an unpaid column. These are the wishes from the same myths, and from the fantasts who followed, that nobody has granted. Some are close, some have a first step and a very long road, and a few have no road that anyone can see.

I find this table the most cheerful one in the article. In 1812, when the Grimms printed a mirror that answered questions, the idea was exactly as absurd as waking the dead is now. In October 1903 a serious newspaper put the flying machine a million years away. Every row above the line in this article once sat below it.

Visual 09  /  The Open Ledger
Wishes from myth and fiction that have not been granted. The gauge has five steps: no known path, theory only, laboratory demonstration, prototype, early product. Sorted from furthest along to least.
A first step is not a promise. Several of these may never arrive, and at least one (travel into the past) has no known route at all.

A whale's click sequence turns out to have structure. A paralysed woman's attempted words come out of a decoder at 78 a minute. A pellet of hydrogen gives back more energy than the laser spent on it. None of these grants the wish. Each stands to it roughly as a paper bag full of hot air over Paris in 1783 stands to the 4.98 billion airline passengers carried last year.

Three are stuck at zero: to wake the dead, to be elsewhere in an instant, and to hear from another world. They are the oldest wishes on the list, and three thousand years of asking has not moved them. I would not bet on them. I also notice that Rutherford would not have bet on the reactor, and he was the best-informed man on Earth on that subject, on the day before it was invented.

Somebody is writing the fairy tales now that our descendants will live inside. Most of them are called science fiction and a few are called research proposals. If the last three thousand years are a guide, the wait is getting shorter, the bill will arrive with the goods, and the people it happens to will mostly complain about the choice of films.

The count

I went looking for evidence that we live in the world our ancestors wished for, and the count came out as follows. Of ten wishes that turn up in the stories of more than forty peoples, eight have been granted outright and are now so ordinary that we are bored by them: flight, sight at a distance, speech at a distance, made servants, the full table, speed and light, the Moon, the library of everything. One, healing, has been granted to a degree no king ever enjoyed and still ends where it always did. One, a life under the sea, we visited and decided against.

The tales predicted none of it. Their use was to keep the list. People told the same ten wishes on every continent for three thousand years, then some of the listeners learned arithmetic, and the wait fell from millennia to a century to the length of a career.

What I take from the audit is a habit. When the glass on the table answers a question, I try to remember that this is the Brazen Head, that a man was once thought a sorcerer for being rumoured to own one, and that a girl in a Russian tale had to wait for her sisters to leave the room before she dared roll the apple round the saucer. Then I ask it something. It would be rude not to.
