# The Wish Ledger: people (scientists, naysayers, fantasts)

Research part compiled 2026-11-24. Method: every claim in the "what" and "quote" columns was checked against a page opened during this session (the URL in the source column). Caveats that apply to the whole file:

- Life dates: taken from the fetched pages where they appeared; for the most famous names (Leonardo, Kepler, Verne, Wells, Huxley, etc.) they are standard reference dates and were not each re-fetched.
- "Arrived" dates marked with (std) are standard reference dates for well-known milestones (e.g. Wright flight 1903, IVF 1978, laser 1960, iPhone 2007) that were not on the page fetched for that row. Re-check them before print if a number is used in the article.
- Quotes from pre-1930 texts are exact as shown on the source page. Post-1930 texts are paraphrased, with at most a few quoted words.
- years_off = year arrived minus year written, unless the author named a target year (then the gap to the target is given in the note).

## Best stories

- Verne's Moon shot against Apollo 11: in 1865 he launched three men from Florida, and in the 1869 sequel they splash down in the Pacific and are found by a US Navy ship. Apollo 11 left Florida with three men in 1969, came down in the Pacific to the USS Hornet, and its command module Columbia was named partly for Verne's cannon, the Columbiad. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11
- Rutherford and Szilard: on 12 September 1933 Leo Szilard read in The Times that Rutherford had called atomic power "moonshine", and conceived the neutron chain reaction the same day while waiting at a London crossing on Southampton Row. He had read Wells's The World Set Free, with its "atomic bombs", the year before, and the first reactor ran on 2 December 1942. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Szilard
- The 49-year correction: on 13 January 1920 The New York Times wrote that Robert Goddard seemed "to lack the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools" for thinking a rocket could work in a vacuum. On 17 July 1969, with Apollo 11 on its way to the Moon, the paper printed "A Correction" that ends "The Times regrets the error." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Goddard
- Sixty-nine days: on 9 October 1903 a New York Times editorial said a working flying machine might take "from one million to ten million years". The Wright brothers flew on 17 December 1903, 69 days later. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Machines_Which_Do_Not_Fly
- Tesla's pocket: in Collier's on 30 January 1926 Tesla said wireless would turn the whole earth into "a huge brain" and that we would see and hear one another as if face to face. He added: "A man will be able to carry one in his vest pocket." https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Nikola_Tesla
- Comte and the stars: in 1835 Auguste Comte chose the chemical composition of the stars as his example of something humans could never know. In 1859 Kirchhoff and Bunsen used spectral lines to show the Sun contains sodium, 24 years later. https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-comte.htm
- Odoevsky's home newspapers: in a Russian text published in 1835 and set in the year 4338, friends' houses are linked by "magnetic telegraphs" and many households issue their own newspapers to acquaintances instead of writing letters. The telephone arrived 41 years later and the blog about 164 years later, not 2,500. https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/4338-й_год_(Одоевский)
- Lovelace's music: in 1843 Ada Lovelace wrote that Babbage's engine "might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent." In the same notes she also insisted that the engine "has no pretensions whatever to originate any thing", the objection Turing answered in 1950. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Scientific_Memoirs/3/Sketch_of_the_Analytical_Engine_invented_by_Charles_Babbage,_Esq./Notes_by_the_Translator
- The legend its own hero retracted: Martin Cooper made the first handheld mobile call on 3 April 1973, seven years after Star Trek's communicator appeared on screen. But in 2021 he said the inspiration "wasn't Star Trek, it was Dick Tracy" and called going along with the Star Trek story a mistake. https://www.goodtimes.sc/cell-phone-inventor-martin-cooper
- Dyson gives the credit back: the "Dyson sphere" comes from Olaf Stapledon's 1937 novel Star Maker, which describes stars wrapped in "a gauze of light-traps". Freeman Dyson wrote in 1979 that he took the idea from Stapledon, and none has yet been found. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyson_sphere
