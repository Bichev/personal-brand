## Best stories

- **The Ebony Horse has a cockpit.** In Burton's translation of the Arabic text the rider finds "the pin of ascent" on the horse's right shoulder and, after a frightening climb with no way down, works out that a matching pin on the left makes it descend. In the Galland and Lang version the control is a peg or screw in the neck, and the prince's mistake is the same: he takes off before the inventor has explained how to land. The tale is older than Galland: an Arabic version sits in the One Hundred and One Nights, whose oldest manuscript is dated 1234 or 1235. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ebony_Horse and https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3439

- **Prince Ali's ivory tube is a telescope that became a video call.** The text describes an ivory pipe about a foot long and as thick as a thumb, "furnished with a glass at both ends", through which you see whatever you wish to behold; Lang's translation calls it a "perspective glass" and a "telescope". The tale reached print through Hanna Diyab, a Syrian from Aleppo who told it to Galland in Paris on 22 May 1709, so the object is an ordinary spyglass with its limits removed. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Blue_Fairy_Book/The_Story_of_Prince_Ahmed_and_the_Fairy_Paribanou and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanna_Diyab

- **The three brothers' gifts only work together.** One brother sees through the tube that the princess is dying, the second flies all three to her on the carpet, the third cures her with the apple. The Sultan cannot decide who deserves her because removing any one device breaks the chain. It reads like a description of remote diagnosis, air ambulance and medicine. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_and_Paribanou

- **The most famous Nights tales are not in the Arabic Nights.** Aladdin, the flying carpet and the spying tube have no Arabic manuscript source; they entered the book in French, from Diyab's telling in 1709. The tales with the most machinery in them (the City of Brass, Abdullah the Merman, the brass boatman) are the ones that really are in the Arabic texts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aladdin and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights

- **A brass signpost you operate by hand.** In the City of Brass, travellers lost in the desert find a brass horseman whose lance carries instructions: rub the rider's hand, he will revolve and stop, then go the way he faces. Later a second brass rider opens a gate when a pin in his navel is turned twelve times. These are written as devices with operating instructions, not as living spirits. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3440

- **Robots guarding relics, with stolen plans.** In the Pali Lokapannatti (11th to 12th century), King Ajatasatru's underground relic chamber is defended by mechanical warriors. The story says the secret of making them belonged to robot makers in "Roma-visaya", the Greek and Roman West, and reached India when a young man from Pataliputta married a master maker's daughter and smuggled the plans home. https://theconversation.com/robots-guarded-buddhas-relics-in-a-legend-of-ancient-india-110078

- **Yan Shi's automaton is taken apart on stage.** In the Liezi, a figure walks, sings in tune and winks at the king's ladies; to save his own life the maker dismantles it and shows leather, wood, glue and lacquer, with a full set of artificial organs. The king tests it: remove the heart and it cannot speak, the liver and it cannot see, the kidneys and it cannot walk. The text is no later than about 370 CE. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Mu_of_Zhou

- **Sanjaya is a live war correspondent.** A sage gives a charioteer sight at a distance so that he can sit beside a blind king and describe, as it happens, a battle fought far away. The whole war section of the Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita, is framed as his running report. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjaya

- **An Egyptian spell for outsourcing labour, nearly 3,900 years old.** From about 1850 BCE Egyptians wrote a spell (Coffin Texts 472) so that a figurine would do the dead person's farm work when called; the spell was later inscribed on the figures themselves. It is the oldest clear record in this region of wanting a made thing to do your work on command. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushabti

- **Wan Hu is a modern legend, and that is the story.** The Chinese official with 47 rockets under his chair has a crater on the Moon, but no Chinese source for him is known before an American magazine piece of 1909 (where he is "Wang Tu", set in 2000 BCE, long before gunpowder) and a 1945 American book. The genuinely old Moon stories here run the other way: Chang'e floats up after drinking an elixir, and Kaguya-hime is a Moon person visiting Earth. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wan_Hu
