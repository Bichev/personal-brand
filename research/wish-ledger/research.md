### Best stories

- **Aristotle saw the labour question coming.** In Politics 1.4 he takes Homer's self-moving tripods and says that if shuttles could weave and plectra pluck the lyre by themselves, master craftsmen would need no servants and masters no slaves. It is a fourth-century BCE philosopher treating a myth as an economic thought experiment. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Politics_(Jowett)/Book_1 and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestus
- **The Russian saucer is a screen.** In the tale Afanasyev reprinted from Bronnitsyn's 1838 collection, a girl rolls a ripe apple around a silver saucer and says a rhyme, and the saucer shows cities, ships at sea, regiments and the stars. Later the tsar is asked what he would like to see. https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/Народные_русские_сказки_(Афанасьев)/Сказка_о_серебряном_блюдечке_и_наливном_яблочке
- **Homer's ships have no helmsman.** King Alcinous tells Odysseus that Phaeacian ships have neither pilots nor rudders: they understand what the crew is thinking, know every city and country, and cross the sea through mist and cloud. One made a trip to distant Euboea and back in a day. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaeacians
- **A Welsh tale about surveillance and a secure line.** In Lludd and Llefelys, written down in the 12th or 13th century, Britain is plagued by the Coraniaid, who hear any word the wind touches. The two kings can only plan against them by speaking through a long bronze horn. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lludd_and_Llefelys
- **Emelya never gets off the stove.** In the Russian tale a lazy peasant spares a pike and gets a phrase that makes things work by themselves. Afanasyev's text has the water buckets walking uphill on their own while the neighbours stare; later the sledge drives without a horse and the stove itself leaves the hut and drives him to the king. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At_the_Pike's_Behest
- **The automation failure is as old as the wish.** Lucian's Lover of Lies, about 150 CE, contains the oldest known sorcerer's apprentice story, 1,650 years before Goethe. The Grimms' porridge pot (1815) is the same bug: the pot cooks on command, nobody remembers the stop word, and the town fills with porridge. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sorcerer's_Apprentice and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_Porridge
- **Andersen sent a man to the Moon at the speed of light.** In The Galoshes of Fortune (1838) a night watchman wearing wishing galoshes idly wishes he could visit the Moon and is carried there at once, where the Moon people doubt that Earth can be inhabited. Lucian had already written a Moon voyage in the 2nd century CE, as open satire. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Galoshes_of_Fortune and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_True_Story
- **Wishes with a catch.** Tithonus is granted immortality without youth and withers forever (Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite); Ilmarinen forges a wife of gold and silver in the Kalevala and finds her cold. The tellers who recorded the wish often recorded the cost alongside it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithonus and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilmarinen

### Best stories

- **The Ebony Horse has a cockpit.** In Burton's translation of the Arabic text the rider finds "the pin of ascent" on the horse's right shoulder and, after a frightening climb with no way down, works out that a matching pin on the left makes it descend. In the Galland and Lang version the control is a peg or screw in the neck, and the prince's mistake is the same: he takes off before the inventor has explained how to land. The tale is older than Galland: an Arabic version sits in the One Hundred and One Nights, whose oldest manuscript is dated 1234 or 1235. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ebony_Horse and https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3439

- **Prince Ali's ivory tube is a telescope that became a video call.** The text describes an ivory pipe about a foot long and as thick as a thumb, "furnished with a glass at both ends", through which you see whatever you wish to behold; Lang's translation calls it a "perspective glass" and a "telescope". The tale reached print through Hanna Diyab, a Syrian from Aleppo who told it to Galland in Paris on 22 May 1709, so the object is an ordinary spyglass with its limits removed. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Blue_Fairy_Book/The_Story_of_Prince_Ahmed_and_the_Fairy_Paribanou and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanna_Diyab

- **The three brothers' gifts only work together.** One brother sees through the tube that the princess is dying, the second flies all three to her on the carpet, the third cures her with the apple. The Sultan cannot decide who deserves her because removing any one device breaks the chain. It reads like a description of remote diagnosis, air ambulance and medicine. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_and_Paribanou

- **The most famous Nights tales are not in the Arabic Nights.** Aladdin, the flying carpet and the spying tube have no Arabic manuscript source; they entered the book in French, from Diyab's telling in 1709. The tales with the most machinery in them (the City of Brass, Abdullah the Merman, the brass boatman) are the ones that really are in the Arabic texts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aladdin and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights

- **A brass signpost you operate by hand.** In the City of Brass, travellers lost in the desert find a brass horseman whose lance carries instructions: rub the rider's hand, he will revolve and stop, then go the way he faces. Later a second brass rider opens a gate when a pin in his navel is turned twelve times. These are written as devices with operating instructions, not as living spirits. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3440

- **Robots guarding relics, with stolen plans.** In the Pali Lokapannatti (11th to 12th century), King Ajatasatru's underground relic chamber is defended by mechanical warriors. The story says the secret of making them belonged to robot makers in "Roma-visaya", the Greek and Roman West, and reached India when a young man from Pataliputta married a master maker's daughter and smuggled the plans home. https://theconversation.com/robots-guarded-buddhas-relics-in-a-legend-of-ancient-india-110078

- **Yan Shi's automaton is taken apart on stage.** In the Liezi, a figure walks, sings in tune and winks at the king's ladies; to save his own life the maker dismantles it and shows leather, wood, glue and lacquer, with a full set of artificial organs. The king tests it: remove the heart and it cannot speak, the liver and it cannot see, the kidneys and it cannot walk. The text is no later than about 370 CE. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Mu_of_Zhou

- **Sanjaya is a live war correspondent.** A sage gives a charioteer sight at a distance so that he can sit beside a blind king and describe, as it happens, a battle fought far away. The whole war section of the Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita, is framed as his running report. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjaya

- **An Egyptian spell for outsourcing labour, nearly 3,900 years old.** From about 1850 BCE Egyptians wrote a spell (Coffin Texts 472) so that a figurine would do the dead person's farm work when called; the spell was later inscribed on the figures themselves. It is the oldest clear record in this region of wanting a made thing to do your work on command. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushabti

- **Wan Hu is a modern legend, and that is the story.** The Chinese official with 47 rockets under his chair has a crater on the Moon, but no Chinese source for him is known before an American magazine piece of 1909 (where he is "Wang Tu", set in 2000 BCE, long before gunpowder) and a 1945 American book. The genuinely old Moon stories here run the other way: Chang'e floats up after drinking an elixir, and Kaguya-hime is a Moon person visiting Earth. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wan_Hu

### Best stories

- **The gods deliberately dim human sight (K'iche' Maya).** In the Popol Vuh the first four men made of maize can see the whole earth, and the gods decide that is too close to being gods themselves. Hurakan breathes a cloud over their eyes so they see only what is near. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popol_Vuh and https://archive.org/details/popolvuhmythica00spengoog
- **The revolt of the tools (K'iche' Maya and Quechua).** The Popol Vuh's wooden people are attacked by their own grinding stones, cooking pots and dogs, each naming how it was mistreated. The Quechua Huarochirí Manuscript, written in Peru around 1600, has mortars and grinding stones turn on people too, and Moche art shows the same scene a thousand years earlier. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huarochir%C3%AD_Manuscript
- **Drums that really did carry speech (Kele, Yoruba, Asante).** Because these languages are tonal, a drum with two or more pitches can reproduce the tone pattern of a sentence; drummers add stock phrases to each word to remove ambiguity, as John Carrington explained in 1949. In the 1730s Francis Moore already noted a Mandinka town drum heard six or seven miles away at night. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_drum and https://archive.org/details/travelsintoinlan00moor
- **A chart of 74 islands drawn from memory (Raʻiātea).** In 1769 the navigator Tupaia drew James Cook a chart of islands within about 2,000 miles of his home; he knew some 130. In 1976 the canoe Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti using only stars, swells, winds and birds. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation
- **The spider who could not keep all knowledge (Akan).** Kwaku Ananse gathers all wisdom into one gourd pot, fails to climb a tree with it, and is corrected by his small child. He smashes the pot in anger and wisdom scatters to everyone. https://archive.org/details/akanashantifolkt0000ratt
- **A warrior in a cloud (Baganda).** Kibuka hovers over the battlefield in a cloud and shoots down at the enemy until archers fire into the cloud and wound him. His shrine and relics were real and are described by Roscoe in 1911. https://archive.org/details/cu31924028599433
- **The Moon's promise, lost in transmission (Nama).** The Moon sends word that people will die and live again as she does; the Hare delivers it as "die and come wholly to an end". A wish for return from death recorded as a garbled message. https://archive.org/details/reynardfoxinsout00bleeuoft
- **Shamans who had been to the Moon and to the sea floor (Iglulik Inuit).** Rasmussen's Iglulik hosts in the 1920s described angakkuit visiting the Moon Spirit's house in the sky and descending to the Sea Woman to calm her and release the seals. https://archive.org/details/intellectualcult00rasm

### Best stories

- Verne's Moon shot against Apollo 11: in 1865 he launched three men from Florida, and in the 1869 sequel they splash down in the Pacific and are found by a US Navy ship. Apollo 11 left Florida with three men in 1969, came down in the Pacific to the USS Hornet, and its command module Columbia was named partly for Verne's cannon, the Columbiad. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11
- Rutherford and Szilard: on 12 September 1933 Leo Szilard read in The Times that Rutherford had called atomic power "moonshine", and conceived the neutron chain reaction the same day while waiting at a London crossing on Southampton Row. He had read Wells's The World Set Free, with its "atomic bombs", the year before, and the first reactor ran on 2 December 1942. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Szilard
- The 49-year correction: on 13 January 1920 The New York Times wrote that Robert Goddard seemed "to lack the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools" for thinking a rocket could work in a vacuum. On 17 July 1969, with Apollo 11 on its way to the Moon, the paper printed "A Correction" that ends "The Times regrets the error." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Goddard
- Sixty-nine days: on 9 October 1903 a New York Times editorial said a working flying machine might take "from one million to ten million years". The Wright brothers flew on 17 December 1903, 69 days later. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Machines_Which_Do_Not_Fly
- Tesla's pocket: in Collier's on 30 January 1926 Tesla said wireless would turn the whole earth into "a huge brain" and that we would see and hear one another as if face to face. He added: "A man will be able to carry one in his vest pocket." https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Nikola_Tesla
- Comte and the stars: in 1835 Auguste Comte chose the chemical composition of the stars as his example of something humans could never know. In 1859 Kirchhoff and Bunsen used spectral lines to show the Sun contains sodium, 24 years later. https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-comte.htm
- Odoevsky's home newspapers: in a Russian text published in 1835 and set in the year 4338, friends' houses are linked by "magnetic telegraphs" and many households issue their own newspapers to acquaintances instead of writing letters. The telephone arrived 41 years later and the blog about 164 years later, not 2,500. https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/4338-й_год_(Одоевский)
- Lovelace's music: in 1843 Ada Lovelace wrote that Babbage's engine "might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent." In the same notes she also insisted that the engine "has no pretensions whatever to originate any thing", the objection Turing answered in 1950. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Scientific_Memoirs/3/Sketch_of_the_Analytical_Engine_invented_by_Charles_Babbage,_Esq./Notes_by_the_Translator
- The legend its own hero retracted: Martin Cooper made the first handheld mobile call on 3 April 1973, seven years after Star Trek's communicator appeared on screen. But in 2021 he said the inspiration "wasn't Star Trek, it was Dick Tracy" and called going along with the Star Trek story a mistake. https://www.goodtimes.sc/cell-phone-inventor-martin-cooper
- Dyson gives the credit back: the "Dyson sphere" comes from Olaf Stapledon's 1937 novel Star Maker, which describes stars wrapped in "a gauze of light-traps". Freeman Dyson wrote in 1979 that he took the idea from Stapledon, and none has yet been found. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyson_sphere

### The life-expectancy pitfall

A life expectancy at birth of about 30 does not mean people died at 30. It is an average pulled down by the deaths of infants and children. In the historical samples collected by Volk and Atkinson, roughly a quarter of babies died in their first year and about half of all children died before 15. Those who got through childhood often lived into their fifties, sixties and beyond.

Verified figures (total expected age at death for someone who has reached the stated age):

|---|---|---|---|
| Sweden, 1751 | 38.4 | 58.4 | https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy-at-age-15 |
| Sweden, 1800 | 32.2 | 56.0 | same |
| France, 1816 | 40.1 | 58.4 (a 65-year-old could expect 76) | same ; https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy |
| England and Wales, 1841 | 41.6 | 58.8 (a 5-year-old: 55) | same ; https://ourworldindata.org/its-not-just-about-child-mortality-life-expectancy-improved-at-all-ages |

Two honest points follow. First, the gap between "at birth" and "at 15" has almost closed, which is the child-mortality story. Second, the opposite mistake is also wrong: adults gained too. A 15-year-old in Sweden could expect to reach 58 in 1751 and 83.5 in 2023, about 25 extra years. In England and Wales a 50-year-old could expect to reach 71 in the mid-19th century and 83 today.

### King stories

- **Nathan Mayer Rothschild, 1836.** He died on 28 July 1836 in Frankfurt, aged 58, of an infected abscess (one account says probably a boil), while there for his son's wedding. The route was about 2,700 miles (4,300 km) each way. His departure and return dates are not recorded, so "well over a year" is safe and anything more exact is not. I did not verify an actual scheduled route or time. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_Musa

- **Albert Alexander, 1941.** The first patient treated with penicillin, an Oxford policeman whose infection began with a sore at the corner of his mouth, improved within a day of the first dose on 12 February 1941. The team ran out of the drug, he relapsed, and he died on 15 March. A reminder that a wish can be granted in the lab years before it is granted to people. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin
- **Light as a luxury.** A thousand lumen-hours, about what one modern bulb gives in an evening, cost 5.4 hours of labour in 1800 and under half a second in 1992. A king in 1300 paid, in today's money, roughly 14,000 times what light costs now. https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/hours-of-work-per-1000-lumen-hours
